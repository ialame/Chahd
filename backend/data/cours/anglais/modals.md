Modal verbs are small helping verbs (can, could, may, might, must, should...) that we put **before a main verb** to add meaning: ability, permission, obligation, advice, possibility or deduction. This unit reviews the modals you need for the Bac and the **subtle differences** between them.

## 1. The Grammar of Modals

\begin{definition}[How modals work]
A modal is always followed by the **base verb** (no *to*, no *-s*, no *-ed*): *She **can swim**.* Modals have **no -s** in the third person and **no past -ed**. Negatives add **not** directly (cannot, must not) and questions invert the modal (**Can** you...?).
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
- He **can drive**. — *not* He cans drive / He can drives.
- They **should study**. — **Must** I leave now?
- We **may not** enter without a ticket.
\end{exemple}

\begin{remarque}
When a modal has no form of its own, we use a **substitute**: *be able to* replaces *can*, and *have to* replaces *must*. This is how we make a future or perfect: *I **will be able to** help* / *I **had to** wait*.
\end{remarque}

## 2. Ability: can / could / be able to

\begin{definition}[Expressing what you can do]
**can** = ability in the present. **could** = ability in the past (general ability). **be able to** is used where *can* has no form (future, perfect, after another modal).
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
- I **can speak** three languages. (present ability)
- When she was five, she **could read** already. (past general ability)
- After the course you **will be able to** code. (future)
\end{exemple}

\begin{remarque}
For a **single success** in the past, use *was/were able to* or *managed to*, not *could*: *The door was locked, but I **was able to** open it* (one specific achievement).
\end{remarque}

## 3. Permission: can / may / could

\begin{definition}[Asking and giving permission]
**can** asks or gives permission informally. **may** is more **formal/polite**. **could** is a polite way to **ask** (but not to give) permission.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
- **Can** I borrow your pen? (informal)
- **May** I come in, please? (formal, polite)
- **Could** I ask you a question? (polite request)
- You **may** leave when you finish. (giving permission)
\end{exemple}

## 4. Obligation: must / have to

\begin{definition}[Expressing necessity]
Both **must** and **have to** express obligation. **must** often shows the speaker's own feeling or a strong personal rule. **have to** usually shows an obligation that comes from **outside** (a rule, the law, circumstances).
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
- I **must** call my grandmother tonight. (I feel it is important)
- Students **have to** wear a uniform. (it is the school's rule)
- You **must** finish this essay. (I insist) / We **have to** pay taxes. (the law)
\end{exemple}

\begin{propriete}[must vs have to]
- **must** = obligation felt by the **speaker** (internal).
- **have to** = obligation from an **external** authority.
- Only **have to** has a past and a future: *I **had to** stay; I **will have to** go*. There is no *musted*.
\end{propriete}

## 5. No Obligation vs Prohibition: don't have to / mustn't

\begin{definition}[A dangerous contrast]
**don't have to** = it is **not necessary** (you have a choice). **mustn't** = it is **forbidden** (you have no choice). They look similar but mean the **opposite**.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
- You **don't have to** come; it is optional. (no obligation)
- You **mustn't** smoke here; it is forbidden. (prohibition)
- We **don't have to** wear a tie. (we may, but it is not required)
- Passengers **mustn't** open the door during the flight. (not allowed)
\end{exemple}

\begin{remarque}
Never confuse the two. *You **don't have to** be quiet* means silence is optional. *You **mustn't** be quiet* would mean silence is forbidden. The meaning is completely different.
\end{remarque}

## 6. Advice: should / ought to / had better

\begin{definition}[Recommending an action]
**should** and **ought to** give **advice** or an opinion (the right thing to do). **had better** gives a **stronger** warning, often with the idea of a bad result if the advice is ignored.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
- You **should revise** every day. (general advice)
- He **ought to apologise** for being late. (it is the right thing)
- You **had better hurry**, or you will miss the bus. (strong warning)
- You **shouldn't eat** so much sugar. (negative advice)
\end{exemple}

## 7. Possibility and Probability: may / might / could

\begin{definition}[Talking about what is possible]
**may**, **might** and **could** all express that something is **possible** — it perhaps will or did happen. **might** often suggests a slightly smaller chance than **may**.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
- It **may rain** this afternoon. (it is possible)
- She **might come** to the party, but she is not sure.
- The keys **could be** in the kitchen. (one possibility among others)
\end{exemple}

## 8. Deduction: must / can't

\begin{definition}[Drawing a logical conclusion]
**must** expresses a **certainty** (we are almost sure something is true). **can't** expresses an **impossibility** (we are almost sure it is **not** true). These are deductions based on evidence, not obligations.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
- The lights are on; someone **must be** at home. (logical certainty)
- He **can't be** hungry; he has just eaten. (it is impossible)
- You have worked all day — you **must be** tired.
- That **can't be** her brother; she is an only child.
\end{exemple}

\begin{propriete}[Two faces of must]
- As **obligation**: *You **must** wear a seatbelt* (you are obliged to).
- As **deduction**: *You **must** be the new teacher* (I am sure you are).
The opposite of deduction *must* is **can't**, not *mustn't*.
\end{propriete}

## Key points

\begin{propriete}[To remember]
- A modal is followed by the **base verb**; it never takes **-s** or **-ed**. Use **be able to / have to** as substitutes.
- **can/could/be able to** = ability; **can/may/could** = permission (may = polite).
- **must** = internal obligation; **have to** = external obligation; only *have to* has past/future.
- **don't have to** = not necessary; **mustn't** = forbidden — opposite meanings.
- **should / ought to / had better** = advice (had better is stronger).
- **may / might / could** = possibility; **must** = certainty and **can't** = impossibility (deduction).
\end{propriete}
