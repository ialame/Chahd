In the Bac exam, the **Writing** task is worth **10 points** — a quarter of the whole paper. You are usually asked to write a short text (about 80–120 words) in a given **genre**: an article, a blog post, an email, or an opinion paragraph. Good writing is not about long, rare words; it is about a **clear plan**, a **logical structure**, and **simple, correct sentences**. This unit shows you how to build a paragraph, how to handle each genre, and which connectors and habits earn marks.

## 1. The structure of a paragraph

\begin{definition}[The four parts]
A well-built paragraph has four moves:
- **Topic sentence:** the main idea, announced in one clear sentence.
- **Supporting ideas:** two or three reasons or details that develop the idea.
- **Examples:** a fact, a personal experience or a figure that proves each idea.
- **Concluding sentence:** a short closing that restates the main idea in new words.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
**Topic sentence:** Reading every day improves a student's life in several ways.
**Support + example:** Firstly, it widens vocabulary; a friend of mine learned hundreds of words simply by reading novels.
**Support + example:** Moreover, it builds concentration, because following a story trains the mind to focus.
**Concluding sentence:** For these reasons, a daily reading habit is a small effort with a big reward.
\end{exemple}

\begin{remarque}
Keep **one main idea per paragraph**. A new idea deserves a new paragraph. A topic sentence that promises one thing and a conclusion that delivers another confuses the reader and loses marks.
\end{remarque}

## 2. Writing an article

\begin{definition}[Structure and conventions]
An article informs or entertains readers of a magazine, newspaper or school journal.
- **Title:** short and catchy, sometimes a question.
- **Introduction:** present the topic and grab attention.
- **Body:** one or two paragraphs developing the main points.
- **Conclusion:** a closing thought, a piece of advice or a call to act.
Tone is **semi-formal**; you may address the reader with *you*.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
**Why Volunteering Changes You**
Have you ever given your time to help others? Last summer, our class cleaned a public park, and the experience taught me more than any lesson. Volunteering builds confidence, because you discover that you can make a real difference. It also creates friendships that last. In short, give a few hours of your week — you may gain far more than you give.
\end{exemple}

## 3. Writing a blog post

\begin{definition}[Structure and conventions]
A blog post is a personal, online text written in a **friendly, conversational** tone.
- **Title:** personal and inviting.
- **Hook:** open with a question or a small story.
- **Body:** share your experience or opinion in short paragraphs.
- **Ending:** invite readers to comment or share their views.
You may use *I*, *we* and *you*, and short sentences are welcome.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
**My First Week Without My Phone**
So, I decided to switch off my phone for seven days — and honestly, it was harder than I expected! At first I felt lost, but soon I started reading again and talking more with my family. By Friday, I felt calmer and more present. Have you ever tried a digital break? Tell me about it in the comments below.
\end{exemple}

## 4. Writing an email

\begin{definition}[Formal vs informal]
The **layout** is the same, but the **language** changes with the reader.
- **Formal** (to a teacher, a manager, an institution): *Dear Mr Brown,* … *I am writing to …* … *I look forward to your reply.* … *Yours sincerely,* — no slang, no contractions.
- **Informal** (to a friend or relative): *Hi Sara,* … *How are you?* … *Can't wait to see you!* … *Take care,* — contractions and friendly tone are fine.
Both have a **greeting**, a **purpose**, a **body**, a **closing line** and a **sign-off**.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
**Formal email**
Dear Ms Carter,
I am writing to request information about the summer language course. Could you please tell me the dates and the registration fees? I would be grateful for an early reply.
Yours sincerely,
Karim Idrissi

**Informal email**
Hi Lina,
Guess what — I passed my driving test! Let's celebrate this weekend. Are you free on Saturday? Drop me a message when you can.
Take care,
Karim
\end{exemple}

\begin{remarque}
Match the **opening and the closing**: *Dear Mr / Yours sincerely* go together; *Dear Sir or Madam / Yours faithfully* go together when you do not know the name. Never end a formal email with *Bye!* or an informal one with *Yours faithfully*.
\end{remarque}

## 5. The opinion / argumentative paragraph

\begin{definition}[Structure and conventions]
Here you must **defend a position** clearly.
- **State your opinion** in the topic sentence (*In my view…*, *I strongly believe…*).
- **Give two or three reasons**, each with an example, linked by connectors.
- Optionally **mention the other side** and answer it (*Although some people think…, I believe…*).
- **Restate your opinion** in the conclusion.
\end{definition}

\begin{exemple}
In my view, students should take part in sport at school. Firstly, exercise keeps the body healthy and reduces stress before exams. Secondly, team sports teach cooperation and respect for rules. Although some argue that sport wastes study time, a short match actually refreshes the mind. For these reasons, I am convinced that sport should have a place in every timetable.
\end{exemple}

## 6. Useful connectors

\begin{propriete}[Linking your ideas]
- **Adding:** firstly, secondly, moreover, in addition, furthermore.
- **Contrasting:** however, on the other hand, although, despite, nevertheless.
- **Giving examples:** for example, for instance, such as, in particular.
- **Showing result:** therefore, as a result, so, consequently.
- **Concluding:** in conclusion, to sum up, all in all, in short.
\end{propriete}

\begin{remarque}
Connectors guide the reader, but do not overload one sentence with two of them. One clear link per idea is enough.
\end{remarque}

## 7. Tips and common mistakes

\begin{propriete}[Before, during and after writing]
- **Plan first:** spend two minutes jotting your main idea and your supporting points before you write.
- **Respect the word count and the genre** asked for — an email is not an article.
- **Vary your sentences** but keep them clear; a short correct sentence beats a long broken one.
- **Re-read at the end:** check verb tenses, subject–verb agreement, spelling and punctuation.
\end{propriete}

\begin{remarque}
**Errors to avoid:** forgetting capital letters and full stops; mixing formal and informal tone; writing one huge block with no paragraphs; using a memorised text that does not answer the question; and repeating the same word instead of using a synonym or a pronoun.
\end{remarque}

## Key points

\begin{propriete}[To remember]
- A paragraph = **topic sentence + supporting ideas + examples + concluding sentence**, one main idea only.
- Each genre has its own conventions: **article** (catchy title, semi-formal), **blog post** (personal, conversational), **email** (greeting/purpose/closing, formal or informal), **opinion paragraph** (state, defend, conclude).
- Match the **opening and closing** of an email; never mix tones.
- Use **connectors** (firstly, moreover, however, for example, in conclusion) to guide the reader.
- Always **plan before** writing and **re-read after** to catch mistakes.
\end{propriete}
